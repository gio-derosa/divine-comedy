1|Between two foods, distant and tempting
2|in one way, first one would die of hunger,
3|before a free man would bring one to his teeth;
4|so would a lamb stand between two cravings
5|of fierce wolves, equally fearing;
6|so would a dog stand between two does:
7|therefore, if I kept silent, I do not reproach myself,
8|by my doubts in one way impelled,
9|since it was necessary, nor do I commend myself.
10|I kept silent, but my desire painted
11|was on my face, and the asking with it,
12|much warmer than by distinct speech.
13|Beatrice did just as Daniel did,
14|lifting Nebuchadnezzar from the anger
15|that had made him unjustly cruel;
16|and said: "I see well how there pulls you
17|one and another desire, so that your concern
18|binds itself so that it does not breathe out.
19|You argue: "If the good will endures,
20|the violence of others for what reason
21|diminishes for me the measure of merit?".
22|Also gives you cause to doubt
23|that souls seem to return to the stars,
24|according to the teaching of Plato.
25|These are the questions that on your will
26|press equally; and therefore first
27|I will treat the one that has more of gall.
28|Of the Seraphim he who most in-Gods himself,
29|Moses, Samuel, and that John
30|whichever you want to take, I say, not Mary,
31|do not have in another heaven their seats
32|than these spirits that just now appeared to you,
33|nor have to their being more or fewer years;
34|but all make beautiful the first circle,
35|and differently have sweet life
36|by feeling more and less the eternal breath.
37|Here they showed themselves, not because allotted
38|this sphere is to them, but to make a sign
39|of the celestial one that has least height.
40|Thus it is fitting to speak to your mind,
41|because only from the sensed does it learn
42|that which it then makes worthy of intellect.
43|For this the Scripture condescends
44|to your faculty, and feet and hand
45|attributes to God and means something else;
46|and Holy Church with human aspect
47|Gabriel and Michael represents to you,
48|and the other who made Tobit healthy again.
49|What Timaeus argues about souls
50|is not similar to what is seen here,
51|because, as he says, it seems that he thinks.
52|He says that the soul returns to its star,
53|believing it to have been cut from there
54|when nature gave it as form;
55|and perhaps his meaning is of another manner
56|than the voice sounds, and it may be
57|with an intention not to be derided.
58|If he means that there return to these wheels
59|the honor of the influence and the blame, perhaps
60|on some truth his bow strikes.
61|This principle, badly understood, twisted
62|once almost the whole world, so that Jove,
63|Mercury and Mars it ran off to name.
64|The other doubt that moves you
65|has less poison, because its malice
66|could not lead you away from me elsewhere.
67|That our justice seems unjust
68|in the eyes of mortals, is an argument
69|of faith and not of heretical wickedness.
70|But because your understanding can
71|well penetrate to this truth,
72|as you desire, I will make you content.
73|If violence is when he who suffers
74|contributes nothing to him who forces,
75|these souls were not excused by it:
76|for will, if it does not will, is not quenched,
77|but does as nature does in fire,
78|if a thousand times violence twists it.
79|So that, if it bends much or little,
80|it follows the force; and so did these
81|being able to flee back to the holy place.
82|If their will had been whole,
83|as held Lawrence upon the grill,
84|and made Mucius severe to his hand,
85|so it would have pushed them back along the road
86|from which they had been dragged, as soon as they were loosed;
87|but so firm a will is too rare.
88|And by these words, if gathered
89|you have them as you should, the argument is canceled
90|which would have troubled you yet many more times.
91|But now there crosses before you another pass
92|in front of your eyes, such that by yourself
93|you would not get out: first you would be weary.
94|I have put in your mind as certain
95|that a blessed soul could not lie,
96|because it is always near the first truth;
97|and then you could hear from Piccarda
98|that the affection for the veil Constance kept;
99|so that she seems here to contradict me.
100|Many times already, brother, it has happened
101|that, to flee peril, against liking
102|one did that which it was not fitting to do;
103|like Alcmaeon, who, begged to do it
104|by his father, killed his own mother,
105|so as not to lose piety became pitiless.
106|At this point I want you to think
107|that force mixes itself with will, and they make
108|it so that the offenses cannot be excused.
109|Absolute will does not consent to the harm;
110|but it consents to it in so far as it fears,
111|if it draws back, to fall into more trouble.
112|So, when Piccarda expresses that,
113|she means the absolute will, and I
114|the other; so that we speak truth together".
115|Such was the rippling of the holy stream
116|that issued from the fount from which every truth derives;
117|such it set at peace one and another desire.
118|"O beloved of the first lover, O divine one",
119|I said then, "whose speech floods me
120|and warms so, that more and more it quickens me,
121|my affection is not so deep,
122|that it suffices to render you grace for grace;
123|but may he who sees and can respond to it.
124|I see well that never is sated
125|our intellect, if the truth does not illumine it
126|outside of which no truth extends.
127|It rests in it, like a beast in its lair,
128|as soon as it has reached it; and it can reach it:
129|if not, every desire would be in vain.
130|There is born because of that, in the manner of a shoot,
131|at the foot of the truth the doubt; and it is nature
132|that pushes us to the summit from hill to hill.
133|This invites me, this reassures me
134|with reverence, lady, to ask you
135|about another truth that is obscure to me.
136|I want to know if man can satisfy you
137|for broken vows with other goods such,
138|that on your scale they may not be slight".
139|Beatrice looked at me with eyes full
140|of sparks of love so divine,
141|that, vanquished, my power turned its back,
142|and I almost lost myself with eyes cast down.
