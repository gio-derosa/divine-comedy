1|"After Constantine turned the eagle
2|against the course of heaven, which it had followed
3|behind the ancient one who took Lavinia,
4|a hundred and a hundred years and more the bird of God
5|at the extremity of Europe kept itself,
6|near to the mountains from which it first came forth;
7|and under the shadow of the sacred feathers
8|it governed the world there from hand to hand,
9|and, so changing, upon mine it arrived.
10|Caesar I was and I am Justinian,
11|who, by will of the first love which I feel,
12|from within the laws drew out the excess and the vain.
13|And before I was attentive to the work,
14|one nature to be in Christ, not more,
15|I believed, and with such faith was content;
16|but the blessed Agapetus, who was
17|highest shepherd, to the sincere faith
18|directed me with his words.
19|I believed him; and what was in his faith,
20|I see now as clearly, as you see
21|every contradiction to be both false and true.
22|As soon as with the Church I moved my feet,
23|it pleased God through grace to inspire me
24|with the high labor, and wholly to it I gave myself;
25|and to my Belisarius I entrusted the arms,
26|to whom the right hand of heaven was so joined,
27|that it was a sign that I should rest myself.
28|Now here to the first question comes to a point
29|my answer; but its condition
30|constrains me to follow with some addition,
31|so that you may see with how much reason
32|moves against the most holy standard
33|both he who appropriates it and he who opposes it.
34|See how much virtue has made it worthy
35|of reverence; and it began from the hour
36|that Pallas died to give it a kingdom.
37|You know that it made in Alba its dwelling
38|for three hundred years and beyond, up to the end
39|when the three against the three fought for it still.
40|And you know what it did from the wrong of the Sabine women
41|to the sorrow of Lucretia under seven kings,
42|conquering round about the neighboring peoples.
43|You know what it did carried by the illustrious
44|Romans against Brennus, against Pyrrhus,
45|against the other princes and allies;
46|whereby Torquatus and Quinctius, who from the lock
47|neglected was named, the Decii and the Fabii
48|had the fame that I willingly embalm.
49|It struck down the pride of the Arabs
50|who behind Hannibal passed
51|the alpine rocks, Po, from which you glide.
52|Under it as youths triumphed
53|Scipio and Pompey; and to that hill
54|beneath which you were born it seemed bitter.
55|Then, near the time when all heaven wanted
56|to bring the world back to its own serene manner,
57|Caesar by the will of Rome takes it.
58|And what it did from the Var up to the Rhine,
59|Isère saw and Saône and Seine saw
60|and every valley whence the Rhone is full.
61|What it did after it came out of Ravenna
62|and leapt the Rubicon, was of such flight,
63|that neither tongue nor pen could follow it.
64|Toward Spain it turned the host,
65|then toward Durazzo, and Pharsalia it struck
66|so that at the hot Nile the grief was felt.
67|Antandros and Simois, whence it had set out,
68|it saw again and there where Hector lies;
69|and badly for Ptolemy then it shook itself.
70|From there it descended flashing upon Juba;
71|whence it turned into your west,
72|where it heard the Pompeian trumpet.
73|Of what it did with the following bearer,
74|Brutus with Cassius in hell barks,
75|and Modena and Perugia were grieving.
76|For it weeps still the sad Cleopatra,
77|who, fleeing before it, from the serpent
78|took death sudden and black.
79|With him it ran as far as the red shore;
80|with him it put the world in such peace,
81|that locked for Janus was his temple.
82|But what the standard that makes me speak
83|had done before and afterward was to do
84|for the mortal kingdom which is subject to it,
85|becomes in appearance little and dark,
86|if in the hand of the third Caesar it is looked at
87|with clear eye and with pure affection;
88|for the living justice that inspires me,
89|granted it, in the hand of him I speak of,
90|the glory of taking vengeance for its wrath.
91|Now here marvel at what I reply to you:
92|afterward with Titus it ran to take vengeance
93|for the vengeance of the ancient sin.
94|And when the Lombard tooth bit
95|the Holy Church, under its wings
96|Charlemagne, conquering, succored her.
97|Now you can judge of those such ones
98|whom I accused above and of their faults,
99|which are the cause of all your evils.
100|The one against the public standard the yellow lilies
101|opposes, and the other appropriates it to a party,
102|so that it is hard to see who most is at fault.
103|Let the Ghibellines do, let them do their art
104|under another standard, for badly follows that one
105|always he who parts justice and it;
106|and let this new Charles not strike it down
107|with his Guelphs, but let him fear the claws
108|which from a higher lion stripped the fleece.
109|Many times already have the sons wept
110|for the fault of the father, and let it not be believed
111|that God will change his arms for his lilies!
112|This little star adorns itself
113|with the good spirits who have been active
114|so that honor and fame might follow them:
115|and when the desires rise up there,
116|thus straying, it must be that the rays
117|of true love rise upward less alive.
118|But in the commensurating of our wages
119|with merit is part of our gladness,
120|because we see them neither less nor greater.
121|Thereby the living justice sweetens
122|in us the affection so, that it cannot
123|ever be twisted to any wickedness.
124|Different voices make sweet notes;
125|so different seats in our life
126|render sweet harmony among these wheels.
127|And within the present pearl
128|shines the light of Romeo, whose
129|great and beautiful work was ill rewarded.
130|But the Provençals who worked against him
131|have not laughed; and therefore he walks badly
132|who makes himself harm from another's good doing.
133|Four daughters he had, and each a queen,
134|Raymond Berengar, and this was done for him by
135|Romeo, a humble and pilgrim person.
136|And then the crooked words moved him
137|to demand an accounting of this just man,
138|who rendered him seven and five for ten,
139|thereupon he departed poor and aged;
140|and if the world knew the heart that he had
141|begging his life crust by crust,
142|much as it praises him, it would praise him more".
