1|O our paltry nobility of blood,
2|if you make people glory in you
3|down here where our affection languishes,
4|it will never be a wondrous thing to me:
5|for there where appetite is not twisted,
6|I mean in heaven, I gloried in it.
7|Truly you are a mantle that quickly shrinks:
8|so that, if one does not add to it from day to day,
9|time goes around it with the shears.
10|With the 'you' which Rome first allowed,
11|in which her family least perseveres,
12|my words began again;
13|whereupon Beatrice, who was a little apart,
14|smiling, seemed like the one who coughed
15|at the first fault written of Guinevere.
16|I began: "You are my father;
17|you give me all boldness to speak;
18|you lift me so, that I am more than I.
19|Through so many streams is filled with joy
20|my mind, that it makes gladness of itself
21|because it can bear it so that it does not break.
22|Tell me then, my dear first root,
23|who were your ancestors and what were the years
24|that were marked in your boyhood;
25|tell me of the sheepfold of Saint John
26|how large it was then, and who were the people
27|within it worthy of the highest seats".
28|As there quickens at the breathing of the winds
29|a coal into flame, so I saw that
30|light shine at my blandishments;
31|and as to my eyes it became more beautiful,
32|so with voice more sweet and gentle,
33|but not with this modern speech,
34|it said to me: "From that day when was said 'Ave'
35|to the birth in which my mother, who is now a saint,
36|was relieved of me with whom she was heavy,
37|to its Lion five hundred fifty
38|and thirty times came this fire
39|to be rekindled beneath its paw.
40|My ancestors and I were born in the place
41|where the last sestiere is first found
42|by him who runs in your annual game.
43|Let it suffice to hear this of my elders:
44|who they were and whence they came there,
45|to be silent is more honest than to discourse.
46|All those who at that time were there
47|able to bear arms between Mars and the Baptist,
48|were the fifth of those who now are living.
49|But the citizenry, which is now mixed
50|with Campi, with Certaldo and with Figline,
51|was seen pure down to the lowest artisan.
52|Oh how much better it would be to have as neighbors
53|those people that I speak of, and at Galluzzo
54|and at Trespiano to have your boundary,
55|than to have them within and endure the stench
56|of the peasant of Aguglione, of that one of Signa,
57|who already for swindling has his eye sharp!
58|If the people who in the world most degenerate
59|had not been to Caesar a stepmother,
60|but like a mother to her son benign,
61|such a one is become Florentine and changes money and trades,
62|who would have turned back to Semifonte,
63|there where his grandfather went begging;
64|Montemurlo would still belong to the Counts;
65|the Cerchi would be in the parish of Acone,
66|and perhaps in Valdigreve the Buondelmonti.
67|Always the confusion of persons
68|was the beginning of the evil of the city,
69|as of your body the food that is added on;
70|and a blind bull falls more quickly
71|than a blind lamb; and many times cuts
72|more and better one than the five swords.
73|If you consider Luni and Urbisaglia
74|how they are gone, and how there go
75|after them Chiusi and Senigallia,
76|to hear how families are undone
77|will not seem to you a new thing nor hard,
78|since cities have their end.
79|Your things all have their death,
80|just as you; but it is hidden in some
81|that last long, and lives are short.
82|And as the turning of the heaven of the moon
83|covers and uncovers the shores without rest,
84|so does Fortune with Florence:
85|so that it should not seem a wondrous thing
86|what I will say of the high Florentines
87|whose fame is hidden in time.
88|I saw the Ughi and I saw the Catellini,
89|Filippi, Greci, Ormanni and Alberichi,
90|already in decline, illustrious citizens;
91|and I saw as great as they were ancient,
92|with him of the Sannella, him of the Arca,
93|and Soldanieri and Ardinghi and Bostichi.
94|Above the gate which at present is laden
95|with new felony of such weight
96|that soon it will be the wreck of the bark,
97|were the Ravignani, from whom has descended
98|the count Guido and whoever the name
99|of the high Bellincione has since taken.
100|He of the Pressa knew already how
101|one should rule, and Galigaio had
102|gilded in his house already the hilt and the pommel.
103|Great already was the column of Vair,
104|Sacchetti, Giuochi, Fifanti and Barucci
105|and Galli and those who blush for the bushel.
106|The stock from which the Calfucci were born
107|was already great, and already were drawn
108|to the curule chairs Sizii and Arrigucci.
109|Oh how great I saw those who are undone
110|by their pride! and the balls of gold
111|made Florence flower in all her great deeds.
112|So did the fathers of those
113|who, whenever your church is vacant,
114|make themselves fat staying in consistory.
115|The overweening breed that turns dragon
116|behind him who flees, and to him who shows his tooth
117|or else his purse, like a lamb is placated,
118|was already coming up, but from little people;
119|so that it did not please Ubertin Donato
120|that afterward his father-in-law made him their kinsman.
121|Already had Caponsacco into the market
122|come down from Fiesole, and already was
123|a good citizen Giuda and Infangato.
124|I will say a thing incredible and true:
125|into the small circle one entered by a gate
126|which was named after those of the Pera.
127|Each one who bears the beautiful ensign
128|of the great baron whose name and whose worth
129|the feast of Thomas keeps fresh,
130|from him had knighthood and privilege;
131|although with the people sides
132|today he who binds it with a border.
133|Already there were Gualterotti and Importuni;
134|and Borgo would still be more quiet,
135|if they were fasting of new neighbors.
136|The house from which your weeping was born,
137|through the just disdain that has killed you
138|and put an end to your glad living,
139|was honored, it and its associates:
140|O Buondelmonte, how wrongly you fled
141|its nuptials at the urging of others!
142|Many would be glad, who are sad,
143|if God had given you to the Ema
144|the first time that you came to the city.
145|But it was fitting, to that broken stone
146|which guards the bridge, that Florence should make
147|a victim in her last peace.
148|With these people, and with others with them,
149|I saw Florence in such repose,
150|that she had no cause for which to weep.
151|With these people I saw glorious
152|and just her populace, so much that the lily
153|was never on the lance placed reversed,
154|nor by division made vermilion".
