1|The many people and the diverse wounds
2|had made my eyes so drunk,
3|that they were eager to stay and weep.
4|But Virgil said to me: "What are you still gazing at?
5|why does your sight still rest
6|down there among the sad mutilated shades?
7|You have not done so at the other pouches;
8|consider, if you think to count them,
9|that twenty-two miles the valley circles.
10|And already the moon is beneath our feet;
11|the time is short now that is granted us,
12|and there is other to see that you do not see".
13|"If you had", I answered thereupon,
14|"attended to the reason for which I was looking,
15|perhaps you would have allowed me still to stay".
16|Meanwhile he went away, and I went behind him,
17|the leader, already making the answer,
18|and adding: "Within that hollow
19|where I was holding my eyes so fixedly just now,
20|I believe that a spirit of my blood laments
21|the guilt that down there costs so much".
22|Then the master said: "Let not be broken
23|your thought from here on over him.
24|Attend to something else, and let him stay there;
25|for I saw him at the foot of the little bridge
26|point at you and threaten hard with his finger,
27|and I heard him named Geri del Bello.
28|You were then so wholly occupied
29|with him who once held Hautefort,
30|that you did not look there, until he was gone".
31|"O my leader, the violent death
32|which is not yet avenged for him", I said,
33|"by anyone who is a sharer in the shame,
34|made him disdainful; whereupon he went away
35|without speaking to me, as I judge:
36|and in that he has made me more compassionate toward him".
37|Thus we spoke as far as the first place
38|which from the crag shows the next valley,
39|if more light were there, all the way to the bottom.
40|When we were above the last cloister
41|of Malebolge, so that its lay brothers
42|could appear to our sight,
43|laments shot at me, diverse,
44|which had their arrows tipped with pity;
45|whereupon I covered my ears with my hands.
46|What pain there would be, if from the hospitals
47|of Valdichiana between July and September
48|and of Maremma and of Sardinia the ills
49|were in one ditch all together,
50|such it was there, and such a stench came out
51|as is wont to come from rotted limbs.
52|We descended onto the last bank
53|of the long crag, still on the left hand;
54|and then my sight was more vivid
55|down toward the bottom, where the minister
56|of the high Lord, infallible justice,
57|punishes the falsifiers whom she registers here.
58|I do not believe that to see greater sadness
59|there was in Aegina the whole people sick,
60|when the air was so full of malice,
61|that the animals, down to the little worm,
62|all fell, and then the ancient peoples,
63|according to what the poets hold for certain,
64|were restored from the seed of ants;
65|than it was to see through that dark valley
66|the spirits languish in different heaps.
67|One upon the belly and one upon the shoulders
68|of another lay, and one on all fours
69|was shifting along the sad path.
70|Step by step we went without speech,
71|looking at and listening to the sick,
72|who could not raise their persons.
73|I saw two sitting leaning on each other,
74|as pan leans against pan to warm,
75|from head to foot spotted with scabs;
76|and I never saw currycomb plied
77|by a stableboy awaited by his lord,
78|nor by one who unwillingly stays awake,
79|as each plied often the bite
80|of his nails upon himself for the great fury
81|of the itching, which has no other relief;
82|and so the nails dragged down the scab,
83|as a knife the scales of a carp
84|or of another fish that has them larger.
85|"O you who with your fingers unmail yourself",
86|began my leader to one of them,
87|"and who make of them at times pincers,
88|tell us if any Italian is among these
89|who are in here, so may your nail suffice you
90|eternally for this work".
91|"Italians are we, whom you see so ruined
92|here both", answered one weeping;
93|"but you, who are you who asked about us?".
94|And the leader said: "I am one who descends
95|with this living man down from ledge to ledge,
96|and I intend to show hell to him".
97|Then the mutual support broke;
98|and trembling each turned to me
99|with others who heard it by rebound.
100|The good master drew wholly close to me,
101|saying: "Say to them what you want";
102|and I began, since he wished it:
103|"So may your memory not steal away
104|in the first world from human minds,
105|but may it live under many suns,
106|tell me who you are and of what people;
107|let your foul and loathsome punishment
108|not frighten you from revealing yourselves to me".
109|"I was of Arezzo, and Albero of Siena",
110|answered one, "had me put in the fire;
111|but that for which I died does not bring me here.
112|True it is that I said to him, speaking in jest:
113|'I would know how to raise myself through the air in flight';
114|and he, who had eagerness and little wit,
115|wanted me to show him the art; and only
116|because I did not make him Daedalus, he had me
117|burned by one who held him as a son.
118|But into the last pouch of the ten
119|for the alchemy that I practiced in the world
120|Minos condemned me, whom it is not permitted to err".
121|And I said to the poet: "Now was there ever
122|a people so vain as the Sienese?
123|Surely not the French so by far!".
124|Whereupon the other leper, who heard me,
125|answered to my saying: "Except for me Stricca
126|who knew how to make temperate expenses,
127|and Niccolò who the rich custom
128|of the clove first discovered
129|in the garden where such seed takes root;
130|and except the company in which squandered
131|Caccia d'Asciano the vineyard and the great woodland,
132|and the Abbagliato displayed his wit.
133|But so that you may know who so seconds you
134|against the Sienese, sharpen your eye toward me,
135|so that my face may answer you well:
136|so you will see that I am the shade of Capocchio,
137|who falsified metals with alchemy;
138|and you must remember, if I eye you well,
139|how I was a good ape of nature".
