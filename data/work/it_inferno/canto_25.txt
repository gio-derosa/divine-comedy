1|At the end of his words the thief
2|raised his hands with both the figs,
3|crying: "Take them, God, for at you I aim them!".
4|From then on the serpents were friends to me,
5|because one wound itself then around his neck,
6|as if it said 'I do not want you to say more';
7|and another around his arms, and bound him again,
8|riveting itself so in front,
9|that he could not give a shake with them.
10|Ah Pistoia, Pistoia, why do you not decree
11|to burn yourself to ashes so that you last no more,
12|since in evildoing you surpass your seed?
13|Through all the dark circles of hell
14|I saw no spirit so proud against God,
15|not he who fell at Thebes down from the walls.
16|He fled so that he spoke no more word;
17|and I saw a centaur full of rage
18|come calling: "Where is he, where is the bitter one?".
19|Maremma, I do not believe, has so many
20|as the snakes he had along his croup
21|up to where our form begins.
22|Upon his shoulders, behind the nape,
23|with open wings lay a dragon;
24|and that sets on fire whoever runs into it.
25|My master said: "This one is Cacus,
26|who, beneath the rock of Mount Aventine,
27|many times made a lake of blood.
28|He does not go with his brothers on one road,
29|because of the theft that he fraudulently made
30|of the great herd that he had nearby;
31|whereby his crooked deeds ceased
32|under the club of Hercules, who perhaps
33|gave him a hundred of them, and he did not feel ten".
34|While he spoke thus, that one ran past,
35|and three spirits came below us,
36|of whom neither I nor my leader was aware,
37|except when they cried: "Who are you?";
38|whereupon our talk stopped,
39|and we attended only to them then.
40|I did not know them; but it followed,
41|as it usually follows by some chance,
42|that one had to name another,
43|saying: "Cianfa, where can he have remained?";
44|wherefore I, so that the leader might stay attentive,
45|put my finger up from my chin to my nose.
46|If you are now, reader, slow to believe
47|what I will say, it will be no marvel,
48|for I who saw it, hardly allow it to myself.
49|As I held my brows raised on them,
50|behold a serpent with six feet darts
51|in front of one, and fastens itself all to him.
52|With the middle feet it clasped his belly
53|and with the front ones took his arms;
54|then it bit both the one and the other cheek;
55|the hind ones it stretched to his thighs,
56|and put its tail between them both
57|and behind along the loins stretched it up again.
58|Ivy was never so rooted
59|to a tree, as the horrible beast
60|through the other's limbs entwined its own.
61|Then they stuck together, as if of hot wax
62|they had been, and mixed their color,
63|neither the one nor the other seemed any longer what it was:
64|as there proceeds before the burning,
65|up along the paper, a dark color
66|which is not black yet and the white dies.
67|The other two were looking at him, and each
68|cried: "Alas, Agnello, how you change!
69|See that already you are neither two nor one".
70|Already the two heads had become one,
71|when there appeared to us two figures mingled
72|in one face, where two were lost.
73|The arms became two out of four strips;
74|the thighs with the legs and the belly and the chest
75|became members that were never seen.
76|Every former aspect there was canceled:
77|two and none the perverse image
78|seemed; and such it went away with slow step.
79|As the lizard under the great lash
80|of the dog days, changing hedge,
81|seems a lightning flash if it crosses the road,
82|so seemed, coming toward the bellies
83|of the other two, a little fiery serpent,
84|livid and black like a grain of pepper;
85|and that part through which is first taken
86|our nourishment, in one of them it pierced;
87|then it fell down before him stretched out.
88|The pierced one gazed at it, but said nothing;
89|rather, with feet fixed, he was yawning
90|just as if sleep or fever assailed him.
91|He looked at the serpent and it at him;
92|the one through the wound and the other through the mouth
93|were smoking hard, and the smoke met.
94|Let Lucan be silent now where he touches
95|on the wretched Sabellus and on Nasidius,
96|and wait to hear what now is shot forth.
97|Let Ovid be silent of Cadmus and of Arethusa,
98|for if him into a serpent and her into a fountain
99|he converts in poetry, I do not envy him;
100|for two natures face to face
101|he never transmuted so that both forms
102|were ready to exchange their matter.
103|Together they answered each other by such rules,
104|that the serpent split its tail into a fork,
105|and the wounded one drew together his footprints.
106|The legs with the thighs themselves
107|stuck together so, that in a little while the juncture
108|made no sign that could be seen.
109|The split tail was taking on the figure
110|which was being lost there, and its skin
111|was becoming soft, and that over there hard.
112|I saw the arms enter through the armpits,
113|and the two feet of the beast, which were short,
114|lengthen as much as those were shortening.
115|Then the hind feet, twisted together,
116|became the member that man conceals,
117|and the wretch from his had put forth two.
118|While the smoke veils the one and the other
119|with new color, and generates the hair above
120|on the one part and from the other strips it,
121|the one rose and the other fell down,
122|not turning away however the impious lamps,
123|under which each was changing muzzle.
124|He who was upright drew it toward the temples,
125|and from too much matter that came there
126|the ears came out from the smooth cheeks;
127|what did not run back and was retained
128|of that excess, made a nose for the face
129|and thickened the lips as much as was fitting.
130|He who lay, thrusts his muzzle forward,
131|and draws back his ears into his head
132|as the snail does its horns;
133|and the tongue, which he had united and ready
134|before for speaking, splits, and the forked one
135|in the other closes up; and the smoke stops.
136|The soul which had become a beast,
137|hissing flees through the valley,
138|and the other behind it speaking spits.
139|Then he turned on it his new shoulders,
140|and said to the other: "I want Buoso to run,
141|as I have done, on all fours along this path".
142|Thus I saw the seventh ballast
143|change and transmute; and here let excuse me
144|the novelty if my pen goes astray at all.
145|And although my eyes were
146|somewhat confused and my mind dismayed,
147|those could not flee so hidden,
148|that I did not discern well Puccio Sciancato;
149|and he was the one who alone, of three companions
150|who came first, was not changed;
151|the other was he whom you, Gaville, lament.
