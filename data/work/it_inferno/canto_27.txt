1|Already the flame was upright and quiet
2|so as to say no more, and already was going from us
3|with the leave of the sweet poet,
4|when another, which was coming behind it,
5|made us turn our eyes to its tip
6|because of a confused sound that came out of it.
7|As the Sicilian bull which bellowed first
8|with the weeping of him, and that was right,
9|who had tempered it with his file,
10|bellowed with the voice of the afflicted one,
11|so that, although it was of copper,
12|still it seemed pierced by pain;
13|so, because of not having way or opening
14|from the start in the fire, into its language
15|were converted the wretched words.
16|But after they had taken their journey
17|up through the tip, giving it that quiver
18|which the tongue had given in their passage,
19|we heard say: "O you to whom I direct
20|my voice and who were just now speaking Lombard,
21|saying 'Now go away, I urge you no more',
22|although I have arrived perhaps somewhat late,
23|may it not irk you to stay to speak with me;
24|see that it does not irk me, and I burn!
25|If you just now into this blind world
26|have fallen from that sweet land
27|of Italy whence I bring all my guilt,
28|tell me whether the Romagnoles have peace or war;
29|for I was from the mountains there between Urbino
30|and the ridge from which the Tiber unlocks itself".
31|I was still attentive and bent downward,
32|when my leader touched me on the side,
33|saying: "You speak; this one is Italian".
34|And I, who had my answer already ready,
35|without delay began to speak:
36|"O soul who are hidden down there,
37|your Romagna is not, and never was,
38|without war in the hearts of its tyrants;
39|but open war I left none there now.
40|Ravenna stands as it has stood many years:
41|the eagle of Polenta broods over it,
42|so that it covers Cervia with its pinions.
43|The city which once made the long trial
44|and of the French a bloody heap,
45|finds itself under the green paws.
46|And the old mastiff and the new of Verrucchio,
47|who made of Montagna the evil disposal,
48|there where they are accustomed make an auger of their teeth.
49|The cities of Lamone and of Santerno
50|are led by the little lion of the white nest,
51|who changes party from summer to winter.
52|And that one whose flank the Savio bathes,
53|just as it sits between the plain and the mountain,
54|lives between tyranny and free state.
55|Now who you are, I pray you to tell us;
56|do not be harder than another has been,
57|so may your name in the world hold its front".
58|After the fire had roared somewhat
59|in its manner, it moved the sharp tip
60|here, there, and then gave forth such breath:
61|"If I believed that my answer were
62|to a person who would ever return to the world,
63|this flame would stay without more shakings;
64|but since never from this bottom
65|has anyone returned alive, if I hear the truth,
66|without fear of infamy I answer you.
67|I was a man of arms, and then I was a corded friar,
68|believing myself, so girt, to make amends;
69|and surely my belief would have come true,
70|were it not for the great priest, may evil take him!,
71|who put me back into my first faults;
72|and how and why, I want you to understand from me.
73|While I was the form of bones and flesh
74|that my mother gave me, my deeds
75|were not those of a lion, but of a fox.
76|The stratagems and the covert ways
77|I knew all, and so plied their art,
78|that to the end of the earth the sound went out.
79|When I saw myself arrived at that part
80|of my age where each one should
81|lower the sails and gather in the ropes,
82|what before pleased me, then grieved me,
83|and repentant and confessed I gave myself up;
84|ah miserable wretch! and it would have availed.
85|The prince of the new Pharisees,
86|having war near the Lateran,
87|and not with Saracens nor with Jews,
88|for each of his enemies was Christian,
89|and none had been to conquer Acre
90|nor a merchant in the land of the Sultan,
91|neither highest office nor sacred orders
92|did he regard in himself, nor in me that cord
93|which used to make those girt with it leaner.
94|But as Constantine sought Sylvester
95|within Soracte to cure his leprosy,
96|so this one sought me as master
97|to cure him of his proud fever;
98|he asked me for counsel, and I kept silent
99|because his words seemed drunken.
100|And then he said again: 'Let your heart not suspect;
101|from now on I absolve you, and you teach me to do
102|how I may throw Palestrina to the ground.
103|Heaven I can lock and unlock,
104|as you know; therefore two are the keys
105|which my predecessor did not hold dear'.
106|Then the grave arguments pushed me
107|there where silence seemed to me the worst,
108|and I said: 'Father, since you wash me
109|of that sin into which I now must fall,
110|long promise with short keeping
111|will make you triumph on the high seat'.
112|Francis came then, when I was dead,
113|for me; but one of the black cherubim
114|said to him: 'Do not carry him; do not do me wrong.
115|He must come down among my wretches
116|because he gave the fraudulent counsel,
117|since which till now I have been at his hair;
118|for one cannot absolve who does not repent,
119|nor can one repent and will at once
120|because of the contradiction which does not allow it'.
121|Oh sorrowful me! how I started
122|when he seized me saying to me: 'Perhaps
123|you did not think that I was a logician!'.
124|To Minos he carried me; and he twisted
125|eight times his tail around his hard back;
126|and after he had bitten it in great rage,
127|he said: 'This one is of the guilty ones of the thieving fire';
128|wherefore I, there where you see, am lost,
129|and so clothed, going, I grieve".
130|When he had thus completed his speech,
131|the flame, sorrowing, departed,
132|twisting and tossing its sharp horn.
133|We passed on, both I and my leader,
134|up along the crag as far as onto the next arch
135|which covers the ditch in which the fee is paid
136|by those who, by sundering, acquire a burden.
