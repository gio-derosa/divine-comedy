1|Now one of the hard margins carries us away;
2|and the smoke of the stream overshadows above,
3|so that from the fire it saves the water and the banks.
4|As the Flemings between Wissant and Bruges,
5|fearing the flood that rushes toward them,
6|make the barrier so that the sea may be driven back;
7|and as the Paduans along the Brenta,
8|to defend their towns and their castles,
9|before Carinthia feels the heat:
10|in such an image were those made,
11|although neither so high nor so thick,
12|whoever he was, the master made them.
13|Already we were from the forest removed
14|so far, that I would not have seen where it was,
15|even if I had turned myself backward,
16|when we met a band of souls
17|who were coming along the bank, and each
18|looked at us as one is accustomed in the evening
19|to look at another under a new moon;
20|and so toward us they sharpened their brows
21|as the old tailor does at the eye of the needle.
22|Thus eyed by such a family,
23|I was recognized by one, who took me
24|by the hem and cried: "What a marvel!".
25|And I, when he stretched his arm to me,
26|fixed my eyes on his baked aspect,
27|so that the burned face did not prevent
28|the recognition of him from my intellect;
29|and bending my hand toward his face,
30|I answered: "Are you here, ser Brunetto?".
31|And he: "O my son, let it not displease you
32|if Brunetto Latini a little with you
33|turns back and lets the file go on".
34|I said to him: "As much as I can, I beg it of you;
35|and if you want me to sit with you,
36|I will do it, if it pleases this one with whom I go".
37|"O son", he said, "whoever of this flock
38|stops at all, lies then a hundred years
39|without fanning himself when the fire strikes him.
40|Therefore go on: I will come at your skirts;
41|and then I will rejoin my band,
42|which goes weeping its eternal harms".
43|I did not dare to descend from the road
44|to go level with him; but my head bowed
45|I held like a man who goes reverently.
46|He began: "What fortune or destiny
47|before the last day leads you down here?
48|and who is this one who shows the way?".
49|"Up there above, in the serene life",
50|I answered him, "I lost myself in a valley,
51|before my age was full.
52|Only yesterday morning I turned my back on it:
53|this one appeared to me, as I was returning into it,
54|and leads me back home by this path".
55|And he to me: "If you follow your star,
56|you cannot fail of a glorious port,
57|if I perceived well in the beautiful life;
58|and if I had not died so early,
59|seeing heaven so benign to you,
60|I would have given you comfort in the work.
61|But that ungrateful malignant people
62|which descended from Fiesole of old,
63|and still holds something of the mountain and of the rock,
64|will make itself, for your good doing, your enemy;
65|and it is reason, for among the sour sorb-apples
66|it is unfitting for the sweet fig to bear fruit.
67|Old fame in the world calls them blind;
68|it is a people avaricious, envious and proud:
69|from their customs see that you cleanse yourself.
70|Your fortune reserves for you so much honor,
71|that the one party and the other will have hunger
72|for you; but far will the grass be from the goat.
73|Let the Fiesolan beasts make fodder
74|of themselves, and let them not touch the plant,
75|if any still springs up in their dung,
76|in which may live again the holy seed
77|of those Romans who remained there when
78|was made the nest of so much malice".
79|"If my request were all fulfilled",
80|I answered him, "you would not yet be
81|from human nature placed in banishment;
82|for in my mind is fixed, and now grieves my heart,
83|the dear and good paternal image
84|of you when in the world from hour to hour
85|you taught me how man makes himself eternal:
86|and how much I hold it in gratitude, while I live
87|it is fitting that in my tongue it be discerned.
88|What you narrate of my course I write,
89|and keep it to be glossed with another text
90|by a lady who will know how, if I arrive to her.
91|This much I want to be manifest to you,
92|provided that my conscience does not chide me,
93|that for Fortune, as she wills, I am ready.
94|Not new to my ears is such a pledge:
95|therefore let Fortune turn her wheel
96|as it pleases her, and the peasant his hoe".
97|My master then on his right
98|cheek turned backward and looked at me;
99|then he said: "He listens well who notes it".
100|Nor for all that do I go speaking less
101|with ser Brunetto, and I ask who are
102|his most known and most eminent companions.
103|And he to me: "To know of some is good;
104|of the others it will be praiseworthy to be silent,
105|for the time would be short for so much sound.
106|In sum know that all were clerics
107|and men of letters great and of great fame,
108|by one same sin in the world defiled.
109|Priscian goes away with that wretched crowd,
110|and Francesco d'Accorso also; and see there,
111|if you had had craving for such scurf,
112|you could have him who by the servant of servants
113|was transferred from the Arno to the Bacchiglione,
114|where he left his ill-stretched sinews.
115|I would say more; but the coming and the speech
116|cannot be longer, because I see
117|there rising new smoke from the sand.
118|People come with whom I must not be.
119|Let my Treasure be commended to you,
120|in which I live still, and more I do not ask".
121|Then he turned back, and seemed of those
122|who run at Verona for the green cloth
123|across the countryside; and he seemed of these
124|the one who wins, not he who loses.
