1|After the greetings dignified and glad
2|were repeated three and four times,
3|Sordello drew back, and said: "You, who are you?".
4|"Before to this mountain were turned
5|the souls worthy to rise to God,
6|were my bones by Octavian buried.
7|I am Virgil; and for no other fault
8|did I lose heaven than for not having faith".
9|Thus answered then my leader.
10|As is one who a thing before him
11|sudden sees whereat he marvels,
12|who believes and not, saying "It is... it is not...",
13|such seemed that one; and then he lowered his brows,
14|and humbly returned toward him,
15|and embraced him there where the lesser takes hold.
16|"O glory of the Latins", he said, "through whom
17|our language showed what it could do,
18|O eternal honor of the place whence I was,
19|what merit or what grace shows you to me?
20|If I am of hearing your words worthy,
21|tell me if you come from hell, and from which cloister".
22|"Through all the circles of the woeful realm",
23|answered he him, "have I come here;
24|power from heaven moved me, and with it I come.
25|Not for doing, but for not doing have I lost
26|seeing the high Sun which you desire
27|and which was late by me known.
28|There is a place down there not sad with torments,
29|but with darkness only, where the laments
30|do not sound as wails, but are sighs.
31|There I stay with the innocent little ones
32|by the teeth of death bitten before
33|they were from human guilt exempt;
34|there I stay with those who the three holy
35|virtues did not put on, and without vice
36|knew the others and followed each and every one.
37|But if you know and can, some indication
38|give us whereby we can come more quickly
39|there where purgatory has its true beginning".
40|He answered: "A fixed place is not set for us;
41|lawful it is for me to go up and around;
42|as far as I can go, as guide I join you.
43|But see already how the day declines,
44|and to go up by night is not possible;
45|therefore it is good to think of a fair resting place.
46|There are souls on the right here apart;
47|if you consent to me, I will lead you to them,
48|and not without delight will they be known to you".
49|"How is that?", was answered. "He who wished
50|to climb by night, would he be hindered
51|by others, or would he not because he could not?".
52|And the good Sordello rubbed on the ground his finger,
53|saying: "See? only this line
54|you would not cross after the sun has gone:
55|not however that anything else gave hindrance,
56|than the nocturnal darkness, to going up;
57|that with inability entangles the will.
58|Well one could with it return downward
59|and walk the slope around wandering,
60|while the horizon holds the day shut".
61|Then my lord, as if marveling,
62|"Lead us", he said, "then there where you say
63|that one can have delight while staying".
64|Little removed had we gone from there,
65|when I perceived that the mountain was hollowed,
66|in the way that valleys hollow them here.
67|"Over there", said that shade, "we will go
68|where the slope makes of itself a lap;
69|and there the new day we will await".
70|Between steep and level was a slanting path,
71|which led us to the side of the hollow,
72|there where more than half dies away the rim.
73|Gold and fine silver, cochineal and white lead,
74|indigo, wood shining and clear,
75|fresh emerald at the moment it is split,
76|by the grass and by the flowers, within that hollow
77|placed, each would be in color vanquished,
78|as by its greater is vanquished the less.
79|Nature had not only painted there,
80|but of the sweetness of a thousand odors
81|made there one unknown and indistinct.
82|'Hail, Queen' upon the green and upon the flowers
83|there sitting singing souls I saw,
84|who because of the valley did not appear from outside.
85|"Before the little sun now goes to its nest",
86|began the Mantuan who had turned us there,
87|"among those do not wish that I guide you.
88|From this ledge better the acts and the faces
89|will you know of each and every one,
90|than in the hollow down among them received.
91|He who sits highest and has the look
92|of having neglected what he ought to do,
93|and who does not move his mouth to the others' songs,
94|Rudolph the emperor was, who could
95|have healed the wounds which have killed Italy,
96|so that late through another she is revived.
97|The other who in appearance comforts him,
98|ruled the land where the water rises
99|which the Moldau into the Elbe, and the Elbe into the sea carries:
100|Ottokar was his name, and in swaddling clothes
101|he was better by far than Wenceslaus his son
102|bearded, whom lust and idleness feed.
103|And that small-nosed one who close in counsel
104|seems with him who has so kind an aspect,
105|died fleeing and deflowering the lily:
106|look there how he beats his breast!
107|The other see, who has made for his cheek
108|of his palm, sighing, a bed.
109|Father and father-in-law they are of the plague of France:
110|they know his life vicious and foul,
111|and hence comes the grief which so pierces them.
112|That one who seems so stout-limbed and who harmonizes,
113|singing, with him of the manly nose,
114|of every worth wore girded the cord;
115|and if king after him had remained
116|the youth who behind him sits,
117|well would the worth have gone from vessel to vessel,
118|which cannot be said of the other heirs;
119|James and Frederick have the kingdoms;
120|of the better heritage none possesses.
121|Rarely rises again through the branches
122|human probity; and this he wills
123|who gives it, so that from him it be asked.
124|Also to the big-nosed one go my words
125|no less than to the other, Peter, who with him sings,
126|whereby Apulia and Provence already grieve.
127|So much is than its seed the plant inferior,
128|as, more than Beatrice and Margaret,
129|Constance of her husband still boasts.
130|See the king of the simple life
131|sitting there alone, Henry of England:
132|this one has in his branches a better issue.
133|He who lowest among these sits on the ground,
134|looking upward, is William the marquis,
135|for whose sake both Alessandria and its war
136|make Monferrato and Canavese weep".
