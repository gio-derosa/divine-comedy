1|Already the angel had remained behind us,
2|the angel who had turned us to the sixth circle,
3|having erased from my face one stroke;
4|and those who have toward justice their desire
5|he had told us are blessed, and his words
6|with 'thirst', without more, completed that.
7|And I, lighter than through the other passages,
8|was going, so that without any labor
9|I was following upward the swift spirits;
10|when Virgil began: "Love,
11|kindled by virtue, has always kindled another,
12|provided that its flame appeared outside;
13|whence from the hour when there descended among us
14|into the limbo of hell Juvenal,
15|who made your affection known to me,
16|my goodwill toward you was such
17|as ever most bound one to an unseen person,
18|so that now these stairs will seem short to me.
19|But tell me, and as a friend pardon me
20|if too much confidence loosens my rein,
21|and as a friend now talk with me:
22|how could find within your breast
23|a place avarice, amid so much wisdom
24|as by your care you were filled with?".
25|These words made Statius move
26|a little to laughter first; then he answered:
27|"Every word of yours is to me a dear sign of love.
28|Truly many times things appear
29|which give false matter for doubting
30|because of the true reasons which are hidden.
31|Your question confirms to me your belief
32|to be that I was avaricious in the other life,
33|perhaps because of that circle where I was.
34|Now know that avarice was removed
35|too far from me, and this lack of measure
36|thousands of moons have punished.
37|And were it not that I set right my care,
38|when I understood there where you exclaim,
39|as if angered at human nature:
40|'Why do you not govern, O sacred hunger
41|for gold, the appetite of mortals?',
42|rolling I would feel the grievous jousts.
43|Then I perceived that too wide could open their wings
44|the hands in spending, and I repented
45|of that as well as of the other sins.
46|How many will rise again with shorn hair
47|through ignorance, which of this sin
48|takes away repentance while living and at the last hours!
49|And know that the fault which rebuts
50|by direct opposition any sin,
51|together with it here dries its green;
52|therefore, if I have been among those people
53|who weep for avarice, to purge myself,
54|for its contrary it has befallen me".
55|"Now when you sang the cruel arms
56|of the double sorrow of Jocasta",
57|said the singer of the bucolic songs,
58|"from what Clio with you touches there,
59|it does not seem that yet had made you faithful
60|the faith, without which doing good does not suffice.
61|If that is so, what sun or what candles
62|dispelled your darkness so, that you directed
63|afterward behind the fisherman your sails?".
64|And he to him: "You first sent me
65|toward Parnassus to drink in its grottoes,
66|and first after God you enlightened me.
67|You did like him who goes by night,
68|who carries the light behind and does not help himself,
69|but after him makes persons wise,
70|when you said: 'The age is renewed;
71|justice returns and the first human time,
72|and a new progeny descends from heaven'.
73|Through you I was a poet, through you a Christian:
74|but so that you may see better what I sketch,
75|to color it I will stretch out my hand.
76|Already the world was all pregnant
77|with the true belief, sown
78|by the messengers of the eternal kingdom;
79|and your word touched on above
80|was in harmony with the new preachers;
81|whence I took the habit of visiting them.
82|They came then to seem to me so holy,
83|that, when Domitian persecuted them,
84|without my weeping were not their laments;
85|and while I was over there,
86|I helped them, and their upright customs
87|made me despise all other sects.
88|And before I led the Greeks to the rivers
89|of Thebes in my poem, I had baptism;
90|but through fear I was a hidden Christian,
91|for a long time making show of paganism;
92|and this lukewarmness the fourth circle
93|made me circle more than the fourth century.
94|You then, who have lifted the covering
95|which hid from me so much good as I speak of,
96|while of the climb we have some remaining,
97|tell me where is our ancient Terence,
98|Caecilius and Plautus and Varro, if you know it:
99|tell me if they are damned, and in which ward".
100|"These and Persius and I and many others",
101|answered my leader, "are with that Greek
102|whom the Muses suckled more than any other ever,
103|in the first circle of the blind prison;
104|often we talk of the mountain
105|which always has our nurses with it.
106|Euripides is there with us and Antiphon,
107|Simonides, Agathon and many other
108|Greeks who once with laurel adorned their brow.
109|There are seen of your people
110|Antigone, Deiphyle and Argia,
111|and Ismene as sad as she was.
112|There is seen she who showed Langia;
113|there is the daughter of Tiresias, and Thetis,
114|and with her sisters Deidamia".
115|Both the poets were now silent,
116|anew intent on looking around,
117|free from climbing and from walls;
118|and already four handmaids of the day
119|had remained behind, and the fifth was at the pole,
120|directing still upward its burning tip,
121|when my leader: "I believe that to the edge
122|our right shoulders we must turn,
123|circling the mountain as we are used to do".
124|Thus custom was there our guide,
125|and we took the way with less misgiving
126|because of the assent of that worthy soul.
127|They went in front, and I alone
128|behind, and I listened to their discourses,
129|which gave me understanding for making poetry.
130|But soon broke off the sweet talk
131|a tree which we found in the middle of the road,
132|with fruits to smell sweet and good;
133|and as a fir tree upward tapers
134|from branch to branch, so that one downward,
135|I believe, so that no person may go up.
136|On the side where our road was closed,
137|fell from the high rock a clear liquid
138|and spread over the leaves above.
139|The two poets drew near to the tree;
140|and a voice from within the foliage
141|cried: "Of this food you will have scarcity".
142|Then it said: "More was Mary thinking how
143|the wedding should be honorable and complete,
144|than of her mouth, which now answers for you.
145|And the ancient Roman women, for their drink,
146|were content with water; and Daniel
147|despised food and acquired wisdom.
148|The first age, as long as gold was beautiful,
149|made savory with hunger the acorns,
150|and nectar with thirst every brook.
151|Honey and locusts were the food
152|which nourished the Baptist in the desert;
153|wherefore he is glorious and as great
154|as by the Gospel is revealed to you".
