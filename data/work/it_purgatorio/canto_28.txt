1|Eager now to search within and around
2|the divine forest dense and living,
3|which to my eyes tempered the new day,
4|without waiting longer, I left the bank,
5|taking the level ground slowly, slowly
6|over the soil which on every side gave off fragrance.
7|A sweet breeze, without variation
8|having in itself, was striking me on the forehead
9|with no greater blow than a gentle wind;
10|by which the leaves, trembling, readily
11|all were bending toward the side
12|where its first shadow the holy mountain casts;
13|not however from their being upright departed
14|so much, that the little birds on the tops
15|would leave off practicing all their art;
16|but with full joy the first hours,
17|singing, they received among the leaves,
18|which kept up a bass to their rhymes,
19|such as from branch to branch gathers
20|through the pine forest on the shore of Chiassi,
21|when Aeolus lets loose the sirocco.
22|Already my slow steps had carried me
23|within the ancient wood so far, that I
24|could not see again where I had entered;
25|and behold further going was taken from me by a stream,
26|which toward the left with its little waves
27|bent the grass which on its bank sprang up.
28|All the waters which are here most pure,
29|would seem to have in them some mixture
30|compared to that, which hides nothing,
31|although it moves dark, dark
32|under the perpetual shade, which never
33|lets sun or moon shine there.
34|With my feet I stopped and with my eyes I passed
35|beyond the little river, to gaze at
36|the great variety of the fresh May-boughs;
37|and there appeared to me, just as appears
38|suddenly a thing which turns away
39|through wonder every other thought,
40|a lady all alone who was going along
41|both singing and choosing flower from flower
42|with which all her path was painted.
43|"Ah, fair lady, who at the rays of love
44|warm yourself, if I am to believe the looks
45|which are wont to be witness of the heart,
46|may it come to you in desire to draw forward",
47|said I to her, "toward this river,
48|so that I may understand what you sing.
49|You make me remember where and what was
50|Proserpina at the time when lost
51|her mother her, and she the springtime".
52|As turns, with soles close
53|to the ground and to each other, a lady who is dancing,
54|and foot before foot hardly sets,
55|she turned upon the vermilion and upon the yellow
56|little flowers toward me, not otherwise
57|than a virgin who lowers her modest eyes;
58|and she made my prayers be satisfied,
59|so drawing near, that the sweet sound
60|came to me with its meanings.
61|As soon as she was there where the grasses are
62|bathed already by the waves of the fair river,
63|of raising her eyes she made me a gift.
64|I do not believe that so much light shone
65|under the eyelids of Venus, pierced
66|by her son against all his custom.
67|She was smiling from the other bank upright,
68|arranging many colors with her hands,
69|which the high land without seed brings forth.
70|Three paces the river kept us apart;
71|but the Hellespont, where Xerxes passed,
72|still a curb to all human prides,
73|did not suffer more hatred from Leander
74|for its surging between Sestos and Abydos,
75|than that from me because it did not open then.
76|"You are new, and perhaps because I smile",
77|she began, "in this place chosen
78|for human nature as its nest,
79|marveling some misgiving holds you;
80|but light is given by the psalm 'You have delighted',
81|which can clear the mist from your intellect.
82|And you who are in front and begged me,
83|say if you wish to hear anything else; for I came ready
84|for every question of yours as far as may suffice".
85|"The water", said I, "and the sound of the forest
86|contend within me with a new belief
87|in a thing which I heard contrary to this".
88|Whereupon she: "I will tell how proceeds
89|from its cause that which makes you marvel,
90|and I will purge the mist which strikes you.
91|The highest good, which alone pleases itself,
92|made man good and for good, and this place
93|gave as a pledge to him of eternal peace.
94|Through his fault he dwelt here little;
95|through his fault into weeping and into toil
96|he changed honest laughter and sweet play.
97|So that the disturbance which below it make
98|the exhalations of the water and of the earth,
99|which as far as they can go after the heat,
100|might make no war upon man,
101|this mountain rose toward heaven so far,
102|and is free of them from there where it is locked.
103|Now because in a circuit all
104|the air revolves with the first turning,
105|if the circle is not broken for it at any point,
106|on this height which is all free
107|in the living air, such motion strikes,
108|and makes the forest sound because it is dense;
109|and the plant struck has such power,
110|that with its virtue it impregnates the air
111|and that then, circling, scatters it around;
112|and the other land, according as it is worthy
113|in itself and in its sky, conceives and bears
114|of diverse virtues diverse trees.
115|It would not then seem a marvel over there,
116|this being heard, when some plant
117|without evident seed takes root there.
118|And you must know that the holy plain
119|where you are, of every seed is full,
120|and has fruit in it which over there is not plucked.
121|The water which you see does not rise from a spring
122|which vapor restores which cold condenses,
123|like a river which gains and loses strength;
124|but it comes out of a fountain steady and sure,
125|which as much by the will of God regains,
126|as it pours out, open on two sides.
127|On this side it descends with a power
128|which takes from one the memory of sin;
129|on the other it restores that of every good deed.
130|On this side Lethe; so on the other side
131|Eunoe it is called, and it does not work
132|unless on this side and that it is first tasted:
133|to all other flavors this is superior.
134|And although well might be sated
135|your thirst even if I revealed no more to you,
136|I will give you a corollary still as a grace;
137|nor do I believe that my speech will be less dear to you,
138|if beyond my promise it extends with you.
139|Those who in ancient times sang in poetry
140|the age of gold and its happy state,
141|perhaps on Parnassus dreamed of this place.
142|Here was innocent the human root;
143|here is spring always and every fruit;
144|this is the nectar of which each one speaks".
145|I turned then wholly back
146|to my poets, and saw that with a smile
147|they had heard the last construction;
148|then to the fair lady I turned back my face.
