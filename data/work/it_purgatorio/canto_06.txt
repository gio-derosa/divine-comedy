1|When the game of dice breaks up,
2|he who loses remains sorrowful,
3|repeating the throws, and sadly learns;
4|with the other goes away all the people;
5|one goes in front, and one from behind catches him,
6|and one at his side brings himself to his mind;
7|he does not stop, and to this one and that one attends;
8|he to whom he holds out his hand, presses no more;
9|and so from the crowd he defends himself.
10|Such was I in that dense throng,
11|turning to them, both here and there, my face,
12|and by promising I freed myself from it.
13|There was the Aretine who from the arms
14|fierce of Ghin di Tacco had his death,
15|and the other who drowned running in pursuit.
16|There was praying with hands held out
17|Federigo Novello, and that one from Pisa
18|who made the good Marzucco appear strong.
19|I saw count Orso and the soul divided
20|from its body through spite and through envy,
21|as it said, not for guilt committed;
22|Pier de la Brosse I mean; and here let her provide,
23|while she is on this side, the lady of Brabant,
24|so that for this she be not of a worse flock.
25|When I was free from each and every one
26|of those shades who prayed only that others pray,
27|so that their becoming holy may be hastened,
28|I began: "It seems that you deny to me,
29|O my light, expressly in a certain text
30|that decree of heaven prayer can bend;
31|and these people pray just for this:
32|would then their hope be vain,
33|or is your saying not well clear to me?".
34|And he to me: "My writing is plain;
35|and the hope of these does not fail,
36|if one looks well with a sound mind;
37|for the height of judgment is not lowered
38|because fire of love fulfills in one moment
39|what he must satisfy who here abides;
40|and there where I affirmed that point,
41|was not amended, by praying, the defect,
42|because the prayer was disjoined from God.
43|Truly on so deep a doubt
44|do not settle, unless she tells it to you
45|who will be a light between the truth and the intellect.
46|I do not know if you understand: I speak of Beatrice;
47|you will see her above, upon the summit
48|of this mountain, smiling and happy".
49|And I: "Lord, let us go with greater haste,
50|for already I do not tire myself as before,
51|and see now that the hill casts its shadow".
52|"We will go forward with this day",
53|he answered, "as far as we can now;
54|but the fact is of other form than you suppose.
55|Before you are up there, you will see return
56|him who already hides himself behind the slope,
57|so that his rays you do not break.
58|But see there a soul which, placed
59|alone, all alone, looks toward us:
60|that one will show us the quickest way".
61|We came to it: O Lombard soul,
62|how you stood haughty and disdainful
63|and in the moving of your eyes dignified and slow!
64|It did not say anything to us,
65|but let us go on, only gazing
66|in the manner of a lion when it rests.
67|Yet Virgil drew near to it, praying
68|that it show us the best ascent;
69|and it did not answer his question,
70|but of our country and of our life
71|inquired of us; and the sweet leader began
72|"Mantua..." and the shade, all withdrawn into itself,
73|rose toward him from the place where before it stood,
74|saying: "O Mantuan, I am Sordello
75|of your land!"; and the one embraced the other.
76|Ah servile Italy, hostel of sorrow,
77|ship without pilot in a great tempest,
78|not mistress of provinces, but brothel!
79|That noble soul was so quick,
80|only at the sweet sound of its land,
81|to make for its fellow citizen there a welcome;
82|and now in you do not stay without war
83|your living ones, and the one gnaws the other
84|of those whom one wall and one moat enclose.
85|Search, wretched one, around the shores
86|your coasts, and then look into your bosom,
87|if any part in you enjoys peace.
88|What avails it that for you refitted the bridle
89|Justinian, if the saddle is empty?
90|Without it the shame would be less.
91|Ah people who ought to be devout,
92|and let Caesar sit in the saddle,
93|if well you understand what God notes for you,
94|look how this beast has become vicious
95|for not being corrected by the spurs,
96|since you put your hand to the bridle.
97|O German Albert who abandon
98|her who has become untamed and wild,
99|and ought to bestride her saddle-bows,
100|may just judgment from the stars fall
101|upon your blood, and be it new and open,
102|such that your successor may have fear of it!
103|For you and your father have suffered,
104|by greed for things over there held back,
105|that the garden of the empire be deserted.
106|Come to see Montecchi and Cappelletti,
107|Monaldi and Filippeschi, man without care:
108|those already sad, and these with fears!
109|Come, cruel one, come, and see the oppression
110|of your nobles, and cure their ills;
111|and you will see Santafiora, how dark it is!
112|Come to see your Rome which weeps
113|widowed and alone, and day and night calls:
114|"My Caesar, why do you not keep me company?".
115|Come to see the people, how they love each other!
116|and if no pity for us moves you,
117|come to be ashamed of your fame.
118|And if it is lawful for me, O highest Jove
119|who were on earth for us crucified,
120|are your just eyes turned elsewhere?
121|Or is it preparation which in the abyss
122|of your counsel you make for some good
123|wholly from our perceiving cut off?
124|For the cities of Italy all full
125|are of tyrants, and a Marcellus becomes
126|every peasant who comes playing the partisan.
127|My Florence, well can you be content
128|with this digression which does not touch you,
129|thanks to your people who so strive.
130|Many have justice in their heart, and late it shoots
131|so as not to come without counsel to the bow;
132|but your people has it on the tip of its mouth.
133|Many refuse the common burden;
134|but your people eagerly answers
135|without being called, and cries: "I take it upon me!".
136|Now make yourself glad, for you have good reason:
137|you rich, you with peace and you with wisdom!
138|If I speak the truth, the result does not hide it.
139|Athens and Lacedaemon, which made
140|the ancient laws and were so civil,
141|made toward living well a small sign
142|compared to you, who make such subtle
143|provisions, that to mid November
144|does not reach what you in October spin.
145|How many times, in the time you remember,
146|law, money, office and custom
147|have you changed, and renewed members!
148|And if you remember well and see light,
149|you will see yourself like that sick woman
150|who cannot find rest upon the feathers,
151|but by turning over wards off her pain.
