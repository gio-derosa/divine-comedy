1|"O you who are beyond the sacred river",
2|turning her speech to me by the point,
3|which even by the edge had seemed to me sharp,
4|she began again, continuing without delay,
5|"say, say if this is true; to so great an accusation
6|your confession must be joined".
7|My power was so confounded,
8|that the voice moved, and was extinguished before
9|it was released from its organs.
10|Briefly she waited; then said: "What are you thinking?
11|Answer me; for the sad memories
12|in you are not yet effaced by the water".
13|Confusion and fear together mixed
14|drove such a "yes" out of my mouth,
15|to understand which there was need of the eyes.
16|As a crossbow breaks, when it shoots
17|from too much tension, its cord and the bow,
18|and with less force the shaft touches the mark,
19|so I burst under that heavy burden,
20|pouring forth tears and sighs,
21|and my voice slackened along its passage.
22|Whereupon she to me: "Amid my desires,
23|which were leading you to love the good
24|beyond which there is nothing to aspire to,
25|what ditches lying across or what chains
26|did you find, for which of passing onward
27|you had thus to strip yourself of hope?
28|And what attractions or what advantages
29|in the brow of the others were shown,
30|for which you had to walk before them?".
31|After the drawing of a bitter sigh,
32|I hardly had the voice which answered,
33|and the lips with effort formed it.
34|Weeping I said: "The present things
35|with their false pleasure turned my steps,
36|as soon as your face was hidden".
37|And she: "If you were silent or if you denied
38|what you confess, not less known would be
39|your fault: by such a judge it is known!
40|But when bursts from one's own cheek
41|the accusation of sin, in our court
42|the grindstone turns itself back against the edge.
43|Nevertheless, so that now you may bear shame
44|for your error, and so that another time,
45|hearing the sirens, you may be stronger,
46|put down the seed of weeping and listen:
47|so you will hear how in the contrary direction
48|my buried flesh ought to have moved you.
49|Never did nature or art present to you
50|pleasure, as much as the fair limbs in which I
51|was enclosed, and which are scattered in the earth;
52|and if the highest pleasure so failed you
53|through my death, what mortal thing
54|should then have drawn you into its desire?
55|Truly you ought, at the first arrow
56|of deceitful things, to have risen upward
57|after me who was no longer such.
58|There ought not to have weighed your wings downward,
59|to wait for more blows, either a young girl
60|or other novelty of such brief use.
61|A young little bird waits two or three;
62|but before the eyes of the fully fledged
63|the net is spread in vain or the arrow shot".
64|As children, ashamed, mute
65|with their eyes on the ground stand, listening
66|and recognizing themselves and repentant,
67|so I stood; and she said: "Since
68|through hearing you are grieved, lift up your beard,
69|and you will take more grief by looking".
70|With less resistance is uprooted
71|a sturdy oak, either by our native wind
72|or by that from the land of Iarbas,
73|than I raised at her command my chin;
74|and when by the beard she asked for my face,
75|well I recognized the venom of the argument.
76|And when my face was lifted up,
77|that those first creatures had ceased
78|from their strewing my eye perceived;
79|and my eyes, still little secure,
80|saw Beatrice turned toward the beast
81|which is only one person in two natures.
82|Under her veil and beyond the river
83|she seemed to me to surpass her former self more,
84|than she surpassed the others here, when she was here.
85|Of repentance so stung me there the nettle,
86|that of all other things whichever turned me
87|most to its love, most became hateful to me.
88|Such recognition bit my heart,
89|that I fell overcome; and what I then became,
90|she knows who gave me the cause.
91|Then, when my heart restored to me outward power,
92|the lady whom I had found alone
93|I saw above me, and she said: "Hold me, hold me!".
94|She had drawn me into the river up to the throat,
95|and pulling me behind her she went
96|over the water light as a shuttle.
97|When I was near the blessed bank,
98|'Sprinkle me' so sweetly was heard,
99|that I cannot remember it, let alone write it.
100|The fair lady opened her arms;
101|she clasped my head and submerged me
102|where I had to swallow the water.
103|Then she took me out, and bathed she offered me
104|within the dance of the four fair ones;
105|and each one covered me with her arm.
106|"We are here nymphs and in heaven we are stars;
107|before Beatrice descended to the world,
108|we were ordained to her as her handmaids.
109|We will lead you to her eyes; but in the joyous
110|light which is within them will sharpen yours
111|the three over there, who gaze more deeply".
112|Thus singing they began; and then
113|to the breast of the griffin they led me with them,
114|where Beatrice stood turned toward us.
115|They said: "See that you do not spare your gaze;
116|we have placed you before the emeralds
117|whence Love once drew his arms at you".
118|A thousand desires hotter than flame
119|bound my eyes to the shining eyes,
120|which only upon the griffin stood fixed.
121|As in a mirror the sun, not otherwise
122|the double beast within them was shining,
123|now with the one, now with the other nature.
124|Think, reader, if I marveled,
125|when I saw the thing in itself stay still,
126|and in its image it was transforming itself.
127|While full of amazement and glad
128|my soul was tasting of that food
129|which, satisfying with itself, makes thirsty for itself,
130|showing themselves of a higher order
131|in their bearing, the other three came forward,
132|dancing to their angelic roundelay.
133|"Turn, Beatrice, turn your holy eyes",
134|was their song, "to your faithful one
135|who, to see you, has taken so many steps!
136|Of your grace do us the grace that you unveil
137|to him your mouth, so that he may discern
138|the second beauty which you conceal".
139|O splendor of living light eternal,
140|who has grown so pale under the shade
141|of Parnassus, or has drunk from its well,
142|that he would not seem to have his mind encumbered,
143|trying to render you as you appeared
144|there where, harmonizing, the heaven overshadows you,
145|when in the open air you unveiled yourself?
