1|"Who is this who circles our mountain
2|before death has given him flight,
3|and opens his eyes at his will and covers them?".
4|"I do not know who he is, but I know that he is not alone;
5|ask him, you who are nearer to him,
6|and sweetly, so that he may speak, greet him".
7|Thus two spirits, one leaning toward the other,
8|were discussing me there on the right hand;
9|then they turned their faces up, to speak to me;
10|and one said: "O soul who, fixed
11|in the body still, toward heaven go your way,
12|for charity console us and tell us
13|whence you come and who you are; for you make us
14|marvel so much at your grace,
15|as a thing requires which never was before".
16|And I: "Through the middle of Tuscany spreads
17|a little river which rises in Falterona,
18|and a hundred miles of course do not sate it.
19|From upon it I bring this body:
20|to tell you who I am, would be to speak in vain,
21|for my name does not yet sound much".
22|"If well your meaning I penetrate
23|with my intellect", then answered me
24|he who spoke first, "you speak of the Arno".
25|And the other said to him: "Why did hide
26|this one the name of that river,
27|just as one does with horrible things?".
28|And the shade which about this was asked,
29|discharged its debt thus: "I do not know; but worthy
30|indeed it is that the name of such a valley perish;
31|for from its source, where so pregnant
32|is the alpine mountain from which Pelorus is cut off,
33|that in few places does it pass beyond that mark,
34|as far as there where it gives itself in restoration
35|of that which the sky dries from the sea,
36|whence the rivers have what goes with them,
37|virtue is as an enemy put to flight
38|by all like a snake, either through misfortune
39|of the place, or through evil custom which goads them:
40|whereby have so changed their nature
41|the inhabitants of the wretched valley,
42|that it seems that Circe had them in pasture.
43|Among brutish hogs, more worthy of acorns
44|than of other food made for human use,
45|it first directs its poor path.
46|Curs it finds then, coming down,
47|snarling more than their power demands,
48|and from them disdainfully it twists its snout.
49|It goes on falling; and the more it swells,
50|the more it finds the dogs becoming wolves,
51|the cursed and ill-fated ditch.
52|Descended then through many deep gorges,
53|it finds the foxes so full of fraud,
54|that they fear no wit that may trap them.
55|Nor will I cease to speak because another hears me;
56|and good it will be for this one, if he still remembers
57|what a true spirit unfolds to me.
58|I see your grandson who becomes
59|a hunter of those wolves upon the bank
60|of the fierce river, and terrifies them all.
61|He sells their flesh while it is alive;
62|then he kills them like an old beast;
63|many of life and himself of honor he deprives.
64|Bloody he comes out of the sad wood;
65|he leaves it such, that from now to a thousand years
66|to its first state it will not grow into a forest again".
67|As at the announcement of grievous harms
68|is troubled the face of him who listens,
69|from whatever side the peril may seize him,
70|so saw I the other soul, which turned
71|was listening, become troubled and become sad,
72|after it had the word gathered to itself.
73|The speech of the one and of the other the look
74|made me eager to know their names,
75|and request for them I made mixed with prayers;
76|whereupon the spirit which first spoke to me
77|began again: "You wish that I bring myself
78|to do for you what you do not wish to do for me.
79|But since God wills that in you shine through
80|so much his grace, I will not be sparing to you;
81|therefore know that I was Guido del Duca.
82|My blood was so burned up with envy,
83|that if I had seen a man become glad,
84|you would have seen me with livid spite overspread.
85|Of my seed such straw I reap;
86|O human race, why do you set your heart
87|there where there is need of exclusion of partners?
88|This is Rinier; this is the glory and the honor
89|of the house of Calboli, where no one
90|has made himself heir since of his worth.
91|And not only his blood has been made bare,
92|between the Po and the mountain and the sea and the Reno,
93|of the good required for truth and for pleasure;
94|for within these bounds it is filled
95|with poisonous stumps, so that late
96|by cultivating now would they disappear.
97|Where is the good Lizio and Arrigo Mainardi?
98|Pier Traversaro and Guido di Carpigna?
99|Oh Romagnoles turned into bastards!
100|When in Bologna will a Fabbro take root again?
101|when in Faenza a Bernardin di Fosco,
102|noble shoot of a little weed?
103|Do not marvel if I weep, Tuscan,
104|when I remember, with Guido da Prata,
105|Ugolin d'Azzo who lived with us,
106|Federigo Tignoso and his company,
107|the house of Traversari and the Anastagi
108|(and the one family and the other is without heir),
109|the ladies and the knights, the toils and the ease
110|which love and courtesy made us desire
111|there where hearts have become so wicked.
112|O Bretinoro, why do you not flee away,
113|since your family has gone away
114|and many people so as not to be guilty?
115|Well does Bagnacavallo, which begets no more sons;
116|and ill does Castrocaro, and worse Conio,
117|which in begetting such counts more entangles itself.
118|Well will do the Pagani, when their demon
119|will go away from them; but not however that pure
120|ever will remain the testimony of them.
121|O Ugolin de' Fantolini, secure
122|is your name, since no more is awaited
123|one who can make it, by degenerating, dark.
124|But go away, Tuscan, now; for now it delights me
125|far more to weep than to speak,
126|so has our talk wrung my mind".
127|We knew that those dear souls
128|heard us going; therefore, by keeping silent,
129|they made us confident of the road.
130|When we had become alone going on,
131|like lightning it seemed when it cleaves the air,
132|a voice which came from opposite saying:
133|'Whoever finds me will slay me';
134|and it fled like thunder which dies away,
135|if suddenly the cloud bursts.
136|When from it our hearing had truce,
137|behold the other with so great a crash,
138|that it resembled thundering which quickly follows:
139|"I am Aglauros who became stone";
140|and then, to draw close to the poet,
141|to the right I made, and not forward, my step.
142|Already the air was on every side quiet;
143|and he said to me: "That was the hard bit
144|which should keep man within his bounds.
145|But you take the bait, so that the hook
146|of the ancient adversary draws you to itself;
147|and therefore little avails bridle or lure.
148|The heavens call you and around you wheel,
149|showing you their eternal beauties,
150|and your eye gazes only at the earth;
151|whence he beats you who discerns all".
