1|At the hour when the daytime heat can no longer
2|warm the cold of the moon,
3|vanquished by earth, and at times by Saturn
4|- when the geomancers their Greater Fortune
5|see in the east, before the dawn,
6|rise by a path which stays dark for it but little -,
7|there came to me in a dream a stammering woman,
8|in her eyes squinting, and on her feet crooked,
9|with maimed hands, and in color pallid.
10|I gazed at her; and as the sun comforts
11|the cold limbs which the night weighs down,
12|so my gaze made ready for her
13|the tongue, and then made her wholly straight
14|in a short time, and her wan face,
15|as love wills, so it colored for her.
16|When she had her speech thus loosened,
17|she began to sing so, that with difficulty
18|from her would I have turned my attention.
19|"I am", she sang, "I am the sweet siren,
20|who the sailors in mid-sea lead astray;
21|so full am I of pleasure to hear!
22|I turned Ulysses from his wandering road
23|to my song; and whoever grows used to me,
24|rarely departs; so wholly I satisfy him!".
25|Not yet was her mouth closed again,
26|when a lady appeared holy and quick
27|alongside me to put that one to confusion.
28|"O Virgil, Virgil, who is this?",
29|fiercely she said; and he was coming
30|with his eyes fixed only on that honest one.
31|He took the other, and in front opened her
32|tearing her garments, and showed me her belly;
33|that woke me with the stench which came from it.
34|I moved my eyes, and the good master: "At least three
35|calls I have given you!", he said, "Rise and come;
36|let us find the opening by which you may enter".
37|Up I raised myself, and all were already full
38|of the high day the circles of the sacred mountain,
39|and we were going with the new sun at our back.
40|Following him, I was carrying my brow
41|like one who has it with thought laden,
42|who makes of himself half an arch of a bridge;
43|when I heard "Come; here one passes"
44|spoken in a manner gentle and kind,
45|such as is not heard in this mortal realm.
46|With wings open, which seemed of a swan,
47|he turned us upward who thus spoke to us
48|between two walls of the hard rock.
49|He moved his feathers then and fanned us,
50|'Those who mourn' affirming to be blessed,
51|for they will have their souls mistresses of consolation.
52|"What is the matter that you keep gazing toward the ground?",
53|my guide began to say to me,
54|when both a little above the angel had climbed.
55|And I: "With such misgiving makes me go
56|a new vision which bends me to itself,
57|so that I cannot part from thinking of it".
58|"You saw", he said, "that ancient witch
59|who alone above us now is wept for;
60|you saw how man frees himself from her.
61|Let it suffice you, and strike the ground with your heels;
62|turn your eyes to the lure which spins
63|the eternal king with the great wheels".
64|Like the falcon, which first looks at its feet,
65|then turns at the call and stretches forward
66|through desire of the food which draws it there,
67|so did I become; and so, as far as is cleft
68|the rock to give way to him who goes up,
69|I went on until where the circling begins.
70|When I was out in the open on the fifth circle,
71|I saw people along it who were weeping,
72|lying on the ground all turned downward.
73|'My soul has cleaved to the pavement'
74|I heard them say with such deep sighs,
75|that the words hardly were understood.
76|"O chosen of God, whose sufferings
77|both justice and hope make less hard,
78|direct us toward the high ascents".
79|"If you come secure from lying down,
80|and wish to find the way most quickly,
81|let your right hands be always on the outside".
82|So prayed the poet, and so answered
83|a little before us was it; whereupon I
84|in the speaking noticed the other hidden one,
85|and turned my eyes to the eyes of my lord:
86|whereupon he assented with a glad sign
87|to what the look of my desire was asking.
88|When I could do with myself as I thought fit,
89|I drew myself above that creature
90|whose words first made me take note,
91|saying: "Spirit in whom weeping ripens
92|that without which to God one cannot return,
93|suspend a little for me your greater care.
94|Who you were and why you have turned your backs
95|upward, tell me, and if you wish that I obtain for you
96|anything over there whence I living came".
97|And he to me: "Why our backs
98|heaven turns to itself, you will know; but first
99|know that I was a successor of Peter.
100|Between Sestri and Chiavari flows down
101|a fair river, and from its name
102|the title of my blood takes its summit.
103|One month and a little more I proved how
104|weighs the great mantle on him who keeps it from the mud,
105|so that feathers seem all the other burdens.
106|My conversion, alas!, was late;
107|but, when I was made Roman pastor,
108|so I discovered the lying life.
109|I saw that there the heart was not at rest,
110|nor could one rise higher in that life;
111|wherefore love of this one was kindled in me.
112|Until that moment a wretched soul and separated
113|from God I was, wholly avaricious;
114|now, as you see, here I am punished for it.
115|What avarice does, here is declared
116|in the purgation of the converted souls;
117|and no penalty has the mountain more bitter.
118|Just as our eye did not lift itself
119|on high, fixed on earthly things,
120|so justice here has sunk it to the earth.
121|As avarice extinguished for every good
122|our love, whereby works were lost,
123|so justice here holds us tight,
124|in feet and in hands bound and seized;
125|and as long as it shall be the pleasure of the just Lord,
126|so long shall we stay motionless and stretched out".
127|I had knelt down and wished to speak;
128|but as I began and he perceived,
129|only by listening, my reverence,
130|"What cause", he said, "has thus bent you down?".
131|And I to him: "For your dignity
132|my conscience rightly stung me".
133|"Straighten your legs, raise yourself up, brother!",
134|he answered; "do not err: a fellow servant am I
135|with you and with the others of one power.
136|If ever that holy gospel sound
137|which says 'They neither marry' you understood,
138|well can you see why I reason thus.
139|Go your way now: I do not wish you to stop longer;
140|for your staying hinders my weeping,
141|with which I ripen that which you said.
142|A niece have I over there who has the name Alagia,
143|good in herself, provided that our house
144|does not make her by example wicked;
145|and she alone over there has remained to me".
